Which geometric augmentations are worth applying to CDA crops

Date: 10/09/2026

GOHREP

Goal:

Decide which geometric augmentations to apply to the medoid CDA crops, and how many at once. An earlier attempt applied a large set of them together and did not improve validation accuracy. This works out which of them could have helped, which could not, and which were actively changing the thing being predicted.

Hypotheses:

  1. Some of the augmentations cannot help, because the crop they produce is indistinguishable from the original in every measurable property of the lesion. A horizontal flip is the clearest candidate: a leaf photographed from above has no canonical orientation, and every one of the twelve Chapter 2 features is a mean, a spread, a count or a threshold over a region, all of which are unchanged when the pixels are permuted.
  2. Some of them change the severity content rather than a nuisance direction. Zoom is the clearest candidate here: enlarging the sampled window adds healthy leaf around the lesion, which changes lesion area, mean greenness over the leaf and the green:red pixel ratio – three of the features that track severity. The label stays fixed, so the augmented crop is mislabelled.
  3. Applying many at once compounds 2. Each transform that shifts severity content adds to the last, so a composite of six is further from its label than any single one.

Rationale:

An augmentation is worth applying only if it produces a crop that is different from the original in ways that do not change the label. That is two separate measurements and they have to be made separately:

  • Displacement – how far the augmented crop moves in the twelve Chapter 2 features, standardised on the real crops and expressed in units of the real within-class spread. Near zero means the augmented copy is, in these terms, the same image again, so a model has nothing new to learn from it.
  • Label preservation – whether the top medoid CNN still predicts the same score for the augmented crop. This is the closest available stand-in for asking a human rater whether the severity changed, and it is measured on the same model that would be trained on the augmented data.

Crossing the two gives the decision directly:

label preserved label changed
displacement ~ 0 nothing to learn – drop it (cannot occur)
displacement large real nuisance direction – keep it relabels the data – drop it

Experimental plan:

  1. Rebuild every training crop from the raw leaf photographs, applying each augmentation alone. Validate that the identity case reproduces the stored crops exactly.
  2. Measure displacement and label preservation for each.
  3. Do the same for a composite drawn from the original grid, to measure what applying several at once costs.
  4. Recommend a set.

Why the crops are rebuilt from the raw photographs. Rotating or shearing an already-cropped image leaves black corners, and shifting or zooming a crop can only pad, never reveal. Sampling from the raw leaf photograph means every output pixel is real leaf. This also matches the intent of the original pipeline, which re-cropped a larger region before transforming.

2. Displacement: which augmentations change anything measurable?

Features are standardised on the identity crops, so one unit is one standard deviation of the real data. Displacement is then divided by the real within-class spread – the mean distance from a crop to its own class centroid – so a value of 1.0 means the augmentation moved the crop as far as a typical crop sits from the middle of its own class.

Code 
# 2.1
F0 = z["feat__identity"]
mu, sd = F0.mean(0), F0.std(0)
sd[sd == 0] = 1.0


def zs(F):
    return (F - mu) / sd


Z0 = zs(F0)
cent = np.stack([Z0[y == c].mean(0) for c in range(7)])
within = np.mean([np.linalg.norm(Z0[y == c] - cent[c], axis=1).mean() for c in range(7)])
between = np.mean([np.linalg.norm(cent[a] - cent[b])
                   for a in range(7) for b in range(a + 1, 7)])
print(f"real within-class spread   {within:.3f}")
print(f"real between-class distance {between:.3f}")

rows = []
for n in NAMES:
    if n == "identity":
        continue
    d = np.linalg.norm(zs(z[f"feat__{n}"]) - Z0, axis=1)
    same = (z[f"pred__{n}"] == z["pred__identity"]).mean()
    rows.append({"augmentation": n,
                 "displacement": d.mean() / within,
                 "label preserved": same,

6.1 Which features a brightness change moves, and why

A global gain multiplies every channel by the same constant. Whether a feature survives that is a property of its arithmetic, not something that needs to be discovered:

  • sep is a ratio of between-class variance to total variance of leaf greenness. Scaling greenness by c scales both by c^2, so the ratio is unchanged.
  • log_gr counts pixels where G >= R. A global gain multiplies G and R alike and cannot reverse an inequality, so the counts are unchanged.
  • frac_g_gt_r is the Otsu lesion area over leaf area. Otsu’s threshold is computed from the data and scales with it, so the lesion mask is unchanged – but the leaf mask uses a fixed cut at mean RGB >= 0.15, which does not scale, so a little leakage is expected.
  • leaf_bright, mean_green and cda_bright are absolute intensities in 0-255 units. They scale directly with c and cannot survive.

6.2 A model built only on the gain-invariant features

If the four features above really are immune to exposure, a model restricted to them should hold its accuracy when the gain changes, and should not drift towards higher scores. Both are testable directly.

7. Interpretation and Recommendation

7.1 The geometry was not the problem

Splitting the composites by whether brightness was drawn separates the two causes cleanly. With brightness absent, label preservation falls gently as geometric transforms stack – 0.833 at two, 0.700 at five, 0.621 at seven – so each extra geometric transform costs roughly three points. With brightness present it sits between 0.406 and 0.464 at every count, and stacking more geometry on top changes almost nothing. Brightness costs about twenty-five points the moment it is included; the geometry costs three points per transform. The earlier attempt was applying 5.69 transforms per image, two thirds of which included brightness.

7.2 Flips and right angles are free, and that is exactly why they are worth applying

Horizontal flip, vertical flip and 180-degree rotation move the crop by 0.000 in all twelve features, and 90 degrees by 0.008. This is not a small measured effect, it is an identity: each of these is a permutation of pixels, and every one of the twelve features is a mean, a spread, a count or a threshold over a region, none of which depends on where in the frame a pixel sits. Nothing measurable about the lesion changes.

Yet the CNN changes its prediction on 17 to 22 percent of crops under exactly these operations. That disagreement is the model contradicting itself, not the data changing. Training on flips and right-angle rotations cannot mislabel anything – the label is provably still correct – and it removes an error mode that costs the model up to a fifth of its answers. These are the cheapest gain available here.

7.3 Small geometry earns its place; large-angle rotation does not

Rotation by 15 degrees, shear at 0.3 and a 20-pixel shift move the crop by 0.14 to 0.19 within-class spreads while preserving the label on 83 to 92 percent of crops. Those are real nuisance directions: the bounding box a rater draws is a judgement, so a crop shifted or slightly rotated is a crop the same rater might plausibly have produced.

The original grid’s 150 and 300 degree rotations are not worth keeping. They achieve the same displacement as a 15 degree rotation (0.184 and 0.187 against 0.136) while preserving the label on 78 and 83 percent against 91.9 percent. A 150 degree rotation is a 180 degree rotation – exact, free, label-preserving – composed with a 30 degree one, so nothing is gained by asking for the large angle directly. Use exact multiples of 90 plus a small angle, and the interpolation cost disappears.

7.4 Zoom changes the quantity it is measuring

Zoom moves the crop further than any other geometric transform (0.58 and 0.62), and section 3 shows where it goes: it moves frac_g_gt_r by 1.12 standard deviations, the largest single-feature displacement anywhere in the table. That feature is segmented lesion area over leaf area, and enlarging the sampled window adds healthy leaf around the lesion, so this is the augmentation changing the thing it is measuring rather than perturbing a nuisance direction. Rater bounding boxes do genuinely disagree, so some zoom is defensible, but at a fraction of the tested magnitude – 10 percent rather than 20.

7.5 Brightness must not be a fixed-label augmentation, and the reason is a confound

Brightness produces the largest displacement in the whole table (0.798 and 0.833) and the worst label preservation (0.432 and 0.567). Section 3.2 shows why it is different in kind from the geometric transforms: it disturbs the severity-carrying features almost as much as the others (ratio 0.897), where zoom’s ratio is 0.457 and the flips’ is undefined at zero.

The mechanism is a confound in the data itself. Three of the twelve features are absolute intensities – leaf_bright, mean_green, cda_bright – and all three correlate with severity (rho 0.454, 0.682, 0.702), because dying tissue really is brighter than healthy leaf. A global gain moves those three by 0.76 to 0.95 standard deviations. Brightening a crop therefore moves it along the same axis that severity moves along. Telling the model the label has not changed is telling it something false, and that is why brightness at 1.0 outscored both 0.8 and 1.2 in the earlier sensitivity run.

7.6 What to do about exposure shift instead

The failure this was meant to defend against is real and reproducible. Applying a 1.2 gain to the training crops moves the top CNN’s mean predicted score from 3.035 to 3.505, and a 0.8 gain moves it to 2.395. Brighter images read as more severe, which is the reported behaviour on an externally supplied test set scored almost entirely as 6.

Rescaling each image to a fixed leaf brightness removes the bias but not the damage. After normalising, the mean predicted score returns to 2.94, so the systematic inflation is gone, but per-image accuracy recovers only from 0.529 to 0.548 against 0.715 on clean crops. Clipping is not the explanation – only 2.6 percent of pixels saturate under a 1.2 gain, and repeating the test without clipping gives 0.549. The reason is the confound again: forcing every crop to the same leaf brightness also erases the part of brightness that is genuine severity.

Restricting the model to gain-invariant features does work. Four of the twelve survive a global gain by construction, and the measurements confirm it: sep (0.055 and 0.058), log_gr (0.055 and 0.040), frac_g_gt_r (0.055 and 0.105) and cda_green (0.190 and 0.181). A probe on those four alone scores 0.455 on clean crops against 0.606 for all twelve – but under a 1.2 gain it scores 0.463 against 0.473, under a 0.8 gain 0.459 against 0.406, and its mean predicted score moves from 3.087 to 3.047 where the full set drifts from 3.032 to 3.517. It gives up 15 points of clean accuracy and buys complete immunity to exposure.

The reason this is possible at all is that gain-invariance and severity-carrying are not mutually exclusive here. log_gr and sep are the second and fourth strongest severity carriers in the whole set (rho 0.823 and 0.757) and are also the two least disturbed by gain. Overall brightness cannot distinguish an overexposed leaf from a dying one; these two can.

The deployable form of this is a two-number check. For an incoming batch, compare leaf_bright against the training distribution and compare sep and log_gr against it. leaf_bright far out with sep and log_gr in place means an exposure shift, and the scores should not be trusted. All three moving together means the leaves really are more affected. This costs one pass of fixed arithmetic and needs no model.

7.7 Recommended augmentation set

apply why
horizontal flip yes zero displacement in all twelve features, yet flips up to 22% of the model’s answers
vertical flip yes same
rotation by 90, 180, 270 yes exact, no interpolation, zero displacement
rotation, small angle (up to 15 deg) yes 0.136 displacement at 0.919 label preservation, the best ratio in the table
shift, up to 10% of the box side yes 0.16 displacement at 0.83, and rater box centres differ from their 3-rater mean by a median 5.0% of the box side
shear, up to 0.3 no measured harmless (0.15 displacement at 0.90), but every leaf here was photographed flat, so there is no oblique viewing for it to model
zoom, 0.8 to 1.2 yes rater box sizes span 0.814 to 1.201 of their 3-rater mean, so this range is the raters’ own disagreement rather than a guess
rotation at 150 or 300 deg no same displacement as 15 deg at 9 points worse label preservation, and 180 deg is free
brightness no confounded with severity; handle by feature choice, not by augmentation

Apply one or two per image, not six. Each additional geometric transform costs about three points of label preservation, and there is no measurement here suggesting a composite of six teaches anything a composite of two does not.

7.8 A caveat on the label-preservation measure

Label preservation is the top medoid CNN agreeing with itself, not a human re-scoring the augmented crop. Where a flip changes the prediction, the model is provably wrong, because the crop is identical in every measured property – that direction of the argument is sound. Where zoom or brightness changes the prediction, the model might be right that the severity content moved, or it might be as brittle as the flip result shows it to be. The feature displacements in section 3 are what settle those cases, because they do not depend on any model, and they agree with the prediction changes throughout.
